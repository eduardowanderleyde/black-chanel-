You cross the street to avoid a stranger talking to no one.

Your feet just change direction. But what would have happened to that same person ten thousand years ago, with no hospital and no diagnosis?

You have probably heard the answer. Ancient people believed madness meant demons, so they drilled a hole in the skull to let the demons out. But the real answer is far stranger.

Start with the problem. Madness leaves no bones. A broken leg can show you how it healed forty thousand years later. A hallucination leaves nothing at all. So how could anyone know?

The first clue looks like a confession. Skulls with holes cut into them have turned up across the ancient world, and many patients survived. The practice is called trepanation, which simply means cutting an opening in the skull. But John Verano, a Tulane University anthropologist who studies these skulls in Peru, doesn't mainly see exorcism. He sees head wounds. Bone presses on the brain. A healer opens the skull to clean the damage. In 2018, Verano and his colleagues found that up to 83 percent of patients in the Inca period survived. During the American Civil War, roughly half died. So what does that hole tell you? Someone was trying to save a life.

At Man Bac, in northern Vietnam, archaeologists found a young man buried about four thousand years ago. He was paralyzed before his teens. He couldn't hunt, farm, or walk. He lived at least ten more years. Someone fed him, every day, in a community with little to spare. Lorna Tilley, who studied him, helped build a field called the bioarchaeology of care, which simply means reading kindness from bones.

But a confused mind is different from a paralyzed body, isn't it? Did that care extend to someone screaming at people who weren't there?

For the closest answer, you have to leave archaeology. In the 1950s, the anthropologist Jane Murphy worked with Yupik speakers in the Bering Sea and Yoruba villagers in Nigeria. A popular idea said mental illness was just a label invented by modern societies. Murphy found the opposite. Both groups already had their own words for it. They described people who talked to themselves and believed what no one else believed. Thousands of miles apart, they were describing the stranger you crossed the street to avoid.

The Yupik had another word, kunlangeta. It meant someone whose mind worked perfectly. He knew what he should do, and he didn't do it. He lied. He cheated. He stole. Murphy asked what his people would do with a man like that. Somebody, one man said, would have pushed him off the ice when no one was looking.

Put those side by side. The person who lost their mind could still belong. The person who couldn't be trusted got the ice.

This wasn't paradise. Restraint happened. Some people were driven out. But look at what Plato wrote in his model laws. A person who is mad must not wander the city. Their relatives must keep them at home, or pay a fine. No doctor. No ward. The problem belongs to the family. Sometimes that was gentle. Sometimes it was a locked room. But they stayed among people who remembered who they used to be.

Today, you have more medicine than any human who has ever lived. And a person can lose their mind with nobody noticing.

Even the voices may be listening. In 2015, the Stanford anthropologist Tanya Luhrmann interviewed people with psychosis in California, Ghana, and India. In California, many described violent commands. In Ghana, many heard God. In India, many heard relatives telling them to finish the chores. It was a small study. But it hints that what a broken mind hears may depend on the world around it.

Tomorrow, you may cross the street to avoid a stranger talking to no one. Your ancestors wouldn't have seen a stranger. They would have known who was talking.
